Rites and Operations

A rite is a procedure performed the same way every time, especially when you are frightened, tired, or certain you know better. That is not a joke about operations. That is what a runbook is for, and it is why both traditions write them down in the imperative and number the steps.

I · The Banishing

performed when: a process is wrong and you do not yet know why

The restart. The most-performed rite in operations and the most frequently botched, because it is performed to relieve the operator's anxiety rather than to fix the fault, and it destroys the evidence on its way past.

  1. Before anything: capture the state you are about to destroy. The log tail, the memory figure, the open connections. A restart is an amnesia you are choosing.
  2. Ask what a restart would actually fix. If you cannot name the mechanism, you are not banishing anything, you are hoping.
  3. Restart exactly one thing. Restarting several removes your ability to attribute the recovery to any of them.
  4. Watch the first unit of real work complete. Not the process appearing in the list - the work. Those are different claims and only one of them is the one you wanted.
  5. Write down what you saw, while it is still true. In an hour you will remember it as tidier than it was.

II · The Shared Road

performed when: you will speak to a distant machine more than once

Open the path once and let everything travel on it. The alternative - opening a fresh connection for each errand, because each errand is small - is the most common way to be locked out of a machine that is in perfect health.

  1. Define the road once, in configuration, with multiplexing and a persistence window. Never in the command.
  2. Send every errand through that definition, including the ones you are sure are too small to matter. They are the ones that trip the limit.
  3. When you are refused, stop. Do not retry, and above all do not retry faster. The refusal is a timer, and every attempt restarts it.
  4. Wait out the ban, then reconnect through the road. The machine was never unwell. You were simply judged to be knocking too often.

This rite is the one most often learned the hard way, and the lesson generalises past any particular protocol: the failure looks exactly like the far end being down, and is entirely your own doing.

III · The Naming of the Unseen

performed when: an alarm names something you cannot find

An observer frequently identifies what it saw by a short prefix of a hash rather than by the name the thing calls itself. Searching your records for that identifier will match nothing, ever, no matter how correct the query - and the query looks so correct that you will run it four or five times before doubting it.

  1. Notice that the identifier is a fingerprint, not an address. Fixed width and hexadecimal is the tell.
  2. Stop querying for it. It is not stored anywhere; it was computed at the moment of the alarm and never written down.
  3. Take your candidate identifiers, hash each one, and compare the prefixes. You are matching forwards, not looking up backwards.
  4. When you find it, record the mapping somewhere durable, because the next alarm will present the same problem and you will have forgotten this.

IV · The Drawn Bow

performed when: releasing a change to something that is running

Everything that can be prepared is prepared; the act itself is a single irreversible moment. The discipline is entirely in the preparation, because once the arrow leaves there is no version of this where you get to think again.

  1. Back up what you are about to change, to a timestamped copy, before you change it. This is the rollback. You will not write a better one under pressure.
  2. Change one thing.
  3. Confirm the running system has the new value, not that the file contains it. A process that captured its environment at start will happily serve you the old one while the file says otherwise.
  4. Send one real request through and read the entire answer.
  5. On any unexpected refusal, roll back first and investigate second.

V · The Reading of the Record

performed when: something has already gone wrong

The log is the only station on the tree that can testify. It is also the first thing sacrificed when disk runs short, which means the record of the incident is most likely to be missing precisely when the incident was caused by the thing that filled the disk.

  1. Read forwards from before the fault, not backwards from the error. The error is the consequence; the cause is above it and quieter.
  2. Find the first anomaly, not the loudest. Loud failures are usually downstream of a silent one several minutes earlier.
  3. Check the kernel's own record as well as your application's. A process taken by the reaper leaves nothing in yours.
  4. Establish the ordering before the causation. Two failures a second apart are usually one failure and its consequence.

VI · The Discipline of the Record

performed: continuously, and it is the one nobody performs

The tradition is unanimous and tedious on this point: keep the record. Every system of practice worth the name requires the practitioner to write down what they did and what followed, in order, at the time, before the memory tidies itself.

The operational version is identical and is resisted for identical reasons. It is dull, it slows you down at the exact moment you feel most urgent, and its entire value accrues to a future person who is not you and cannot thank you.

  • Write at the time. A note written an hour later is a reconstruction, and it will be confidently, invisibly wrong about the ordering.
  • Write what you saw, separately from what you concluded. The observation stays true when the conclusion turns out to be mistaken.
  • Write the things that did not work. They are most of what you learned and the first thing dropped from the summary.
  • Write the baseline while it is healthy. Normal latency, normal backlog depth, normal spend. You cannot take that measurement during an incident, which is the only time you will ever want it.
