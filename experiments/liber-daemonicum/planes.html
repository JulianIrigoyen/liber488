Doctrine IV · the planes

The Planes and Their Routes

Of all the stations on the tree, the model is the only one you did not build. You reach it by a name. What answers to that name is chosen by someone else, may be replaced without your knowledge, and is permitted to you by a tier you are not always looking at.

The tradition would call these planes: the same act performed at a different level of the world, subject to different laws, with the practitioner frequently unaware of which one they are operating on. That is an unusually accurate description of routing a request through a subscription rather than a metered key.

The first law of the planes

A refusal is not an outage. When a request that worked yesterday returns a hard error today, the overwhelming likelihood is that something on your side moved: a route changed, a credential expired, a tier does not permit what you asked for on the path you asked for it.

The tell is the shape of the failure. An outage is slow, intermittent, and affects requests that previously differed from each other. A tier refusal is instant, total, and perfectly reproducible - the same request, every time, refused at the same point, while a neighbouring route on the same credential answers normally.

The characteristic mistake: concluding the provider is down because one route returns a hard error, when the adjacent route on the same credential is answering. If one name works and another does not, that is not an outage. That is a permission, and it is yours to read.

The rite of changing a route

Never on a live brain without a way back

Changing which name a running agent calls is a one-line edit with a whole-system blast radius, and it is the single most tempting change to make casually because it looks so small. Treat it as a deploy, because it is one.

  1. Copy the configuration to a timestamped backup before touching it. The backup is the rollback, and you will not write one afterwards.
  2. Change one name. Not two, and not a name and a setting.
  3. Restart, and confirm the new value is what the process actually has - not what the file says. A manager that pinned the environment at start will keep serving you the old one.
  4. Put one real request through and read the whole answer, not just the status.
  5. On any refusal you did not expect, roll back first and investigate second. The investigation is cheaper when the system is serving.

The thing worth internalising

The newest name is not automatically available to you. Availability is a function of your tier, your billing relationship and your data settings, not of what was announced. A name can be entirely real, entirely current, and entirely refused on your particular path.

When that happens the error will often not say so. It will say something generic about the provider. The way you tell the difference is to send the same request down a route you know works: if that answers, the problem is the permission, not the plane.

And when you find the working route, write down why it is the working route. Next year someone will try to upgrade it, for excellent reasons, and rediscover the refusal from scratch.
