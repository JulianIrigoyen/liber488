Library and Sources

What this cabinet borrowed, how it reads, and a plain statement of which half is a joke.

How this cabinet reads

The conceit is a single observation, taken seriously for as long as it holds: the vocabulary of systems administration was borrowed from demonology on purpose, and the borrowing was apt. We summon processes. We bind them with configuration. We give them names they answer to. We are careful about what we grant them, because a thing granted too much does damage that is difficult to undo. When one misbehaves we banish it, and it comes back, because something above it is bound to restore it.

Where the mapping holds, this cabinet follows it. Where it stops holding, the cabinet says so rather than stretching it - a metaphor pushed past its evidence is exactly the failure the occult revival is most often and most fairly accused of.

The structural borrowing is from the grimoire format rather than from any grimoire's content: the ranked hierarchy with a caution column, the sixty-four-fold division of a binary figure, the ten-station tree on three pillars. Those are organising forms, several centuries old and common property. Everything filling them here - the roster, the sixty-four judgments, the stations, the rites - was written for this site.

The forms borrowed

  • The ranked hierarchy with a caution. From the goetic grimoire tradition, whose entries give rank, office, legions, form, and what goes wrong. The last column is the one worth stealing.
  • Sixty-four states of a six-line figure. From the Chinese classic of changes, which worked out that six binary lines give sixty-four cases and that each deserves its own entry - arriving at the insight some three thousand years before anyone had a health check to apply it to.
  • Ten stations on three pillars. From the kabbalistic tree, whose real contribution is structural: that a system is best read as a tension between an expansive side and a restrictive one, resolved down a middle.
  • The sigil from a struck-through sentence. From the nineteenth-century sigil-drawing method, here joined by two methods a machine would prefer.

A word of caution

The framing is a joke. The operational content is not.

Every caution in the hierarchy, every counsel in the sixty-four, and every rite describes a real failure mode of real systems. They are written in this voice because the voice makes them memorable, which is the entire argument for putting a runbook in a costume.

None of it is a substitute for your own logs, your provider's documentation, or your own postmortems. No page here will tell you what your system is doing; only your system can do that, and this cabinet's most repeated counsel is to go and read it rather than reason from first principles about what it probably says.

And the esoteric material is treated here as a source of organising forms and good metaphors, with no claim made on its behalf about the world. Where a tradition makes a factual claim, this cabinet leaves it standing as a claim.

On the seals

Each is computed at page load from the name it belongs to: the string is hashed, and the digest walked around sixteen stations, one per hex digit, stepping inward each time a station repeats. Nothing is stored and nothing is fetched.

A name with high entropy draws a wide, open figure. A name with heavy repetition draws inward spirals. That is the only correspondence this site claims, and it is a true one.
